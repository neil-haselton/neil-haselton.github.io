FRC 2025 Robot

FIRST Robotics Team 2137's robot for the 2025 season, Guillotine

Robot Overview

The 2025 FIRST Robotics Competition game challenged teams to score 11.875” long, 4.5” OD PVC pipes called “coral” onto large structures of steel pipe called reefs. Scoring higher on the reef was worth more points. Additionally, 16.25” inflatable kickballs called “algae” were taken off the reef and scored in nets 6 feet from the ground. At the end of the game, robots climbed onto a cage: a metal frame hanging by a chain under the net.

Our robot was able to complete every task in this game with an extremely simple robot. This simplicity allowed the team to focus on software and driver performance, and we won our first district event since 2019. At the FIRST Championship, we ended third in our division. Statistical rankings commonly had our robot in the top 15 of 526 teams in Michigan. Additionally, the final iteration of our robot, which competed in two offseason events, had a win-loss record of 30-1.

Planning Ahead

When discussing strategy at the start of the season, we weren’t sure if algae would be an important part of the game or not. It required much more complexity than scoring coral, and was worth fewer points. However, I anticipated the possibility that we were wrong, and designed our algae removal mechanism in a way that would make it easy to switch to an algae claw if that were needed. The algae removal mechanism was significantly over-built, but in the end it was worth it, as algae became one of the most important parts of the game. After reefs were full, algae became a limited resource worth more points than coral. This early season decision let us expand our capabilities and stay competitive late into the season.

Climber Iterations

Packaging a climb mechanism into the robot was a difficult challenge. Here are our primary iterations.

Our first iteration was made by my classmate. It was too big, had tolerance issues with gears, and hit the elevator before the robot left the ground.

The second iteration involved a full redesign. It was much simpler, using a spool to pull back the climbing arm rather than gears and chains. However, the robot slipped on the cage, preventing us from lifting off the ground.

The third iteration was the first one that worked. It passively grabbed onto the back of the cage, but required precise alignment and often slipped or went into the cage diagonally. This iteration competed at our first event.

The fourth iteration was similar to the third, but grabbed the inside of the cage. While slipping was still an issue, it was impossible to get it stuck in the cage. This iteration was on our robot for the rest of the season due to time constraints and other priorities.

After having an inconsistent climb all season, we decided to redesign it for our offseason events. The final iteration of our climber has a funnel and two active polyurethane wheels to align the cage and pull it in. After that, two small latches would latch onto the vertical poles of the cage, and we'd pull the climber down to climb. This iteration also featured a ratchet to prevent the climb from falling after the match. After a few small issues at our first offseason event that we fixed, this climb worked every time, as long as we gave ourselves enough time to climb.
